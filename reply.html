Points from your proposal

1. Capital priority

I agree with the 50/50 common equity sitting behind the capital priority. On the rate, I propose distinguishing between two types of capital, based on what each one does for the business:

  • 30% annual preferred return on capital that unlocks more debt, such as your personal equity. This capital is the base that lets us borrow more, so it grows the business beyond its own amount.
  • 20% annual preferred return on capital that requires collateral and does not unlock more debt, such as the SBA loan and merchant financing.

The return then follows the value each type of capital brings: the capital that expands our borrowing capacity earns the higher rate. It also keeps us both incentivized to grow the business and deploy capital efficiently, without our interests drifting apart.

2. Shortfalls

I agree with a carryforward, and I propose limiting it to one year. The shortfall is measured against the rates in point 1: 30% on capital that unlocks more debt and 20% on capital that requires collateral and does not. If a year falls short of those returns, the gap carries forward into the following year only. That gives the capital solid protection in a weak year while keeping the economics balanced.

3. Compensation

I'd propose starting at $17K per month, with the potential to increase to $30K as the business grows, depending on performance and the capital we're able to deploy.

For the profits beyond that, instead of a fixed distribution, I propose a mechanism that lets each of us decide how much of our own share to take out and how much to leave in the business as capital. Each of us can draw what we need during the year, within liquidity limits we agree on, and whatever we leave in keeps working for the business.

Your questions

a, b, c. The rebuild, the license, and the option to buy the IP

The idea of rebuilding the software inside the new company was mainly to avoid unnecessary tax liabilities. I'm equally prepared to grant the new company an exclusive, perpetual, unlimited, and irrevocable license to SellZen, subject to you committing your assets to the new business with a similar level of commitment and protection. The option to buy the existing IP was meant as an additional long-term safeguard for both of us and for the new entity.

The tricky part is tax. Even if I work exclusively for a US company, Israeli tax authorities could treat IP developed by me in Israel as subject to Israeli taxation. I'm working with a tax advisor on how to structure the development, ownership, and transfer of the IP properly.

Another option, and probably the cleanest one, is simply for my existing company to license the software to the new company.

If we sell, we would need to discuss how to wrap the whole deal and do it properly. In any case, a sale would be a bundle: the combined business would be sold together with, most likely, my company that currently owns the IP.

d. Vesting and tax

Yes. One-sided vesting would trigger a valuation of my contribution against what I receive, and if the authorities decide there is a gain, I would be taxed on it. Since the business is in its growth stage and should generate substantial returns right away, the shares would already be worth a lot at each vesting date, so the tax could be significant. That said, tax is not the only reason for my preference.

We're both bringing substantial, proven assets into this partnership: you bring a working operation, and I bring working software. This isn't a startup where equity has to be earned over time. The joint business is entering its growth and scaling stage. As I see it, either we both contribute them from day one and earn our equity immediately, or we both contribute gradually and earn equity as we go, with milestones and protections on both sides. I prefer the first: it's simpler, and the assets we each commit are a stronger guarantee than a vesting schedule. Vesting would make our deal more complicated, prolong the negotiation, and expose us to financial risks.

On the other hand, I understand that doing things step by step is your preferred way, so I'm absolutely open to it as well.

e. If either of us leaves early

If we both commit our assets from day one, our equity reflects those contributions, so it stays with each of us even if one of us leaves.

But if one of us does leave, the one who stays needs a real incentive to keep running and growing the business. We should think about how salary and performance-based pay would work in that case.

I think the chance of either of us walking away in the first year or two is very low, but I agree we need a fair mechanism in case it happens. What specific risks are you trying to protect against? I think we can address them without giving up equal ownership from day one.

f. Financing contingencies

I understand third-party financing is never guaranteed, and I don't expect you to guarantee it. Still, I see financing as one of the key assets you bring to the partnership, and the amount matters a great deal: $4M versus $15M in working capital leads to a substantially different outcome for the business.

So I'd like us to discuss this in more detail, ideally on a call since you have far more experience here. Two things I'd like to understand better:

  • The personal equity you plan to contribute: expected amount and timing.
  • The third-party financing: how confident we can be about the amounts, and what happens if we secure less than expected.

With that picture, we can agree together on reasonable contingencies for the case where the capital comes in lower than planned.
